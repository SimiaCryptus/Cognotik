## ## The Last Sacrament

**Act 1, Scene 1**

**Setting:** subterranean_vault

**Characters:** Elara Vance, Unit 734 (“The Collector”)

---

The silence in the subterranean vault was a pressurized weight—the sound of four centuries of human thought held in stasis, now reduced to a single, final heartbeat.

Elara Vance sat at the mahogany restoration bench, a lone island of wood in a sea of polymer and light-glass. Before her lay a mid-twentieth-century diary. Its burgundy leather was bruised and cracked at the spine like the skin of an elder. With a soft-bristled brush, Elara swept away a microscopic layer of dust. This was the last one: the final physical vessel of a private life not yet converted into binary code.

She turned a page, the vellum protesting with a dry crackle. Tucked near the center was a pressed larkspur, its petals faded to the color of a bruised sky. Beneath it, a single word was written in fountain pen ink, the blue long since turned to a ghostly grey: *Always.*

The ink was slightly blurred at the edge of the ‘A’—the telltale bloom of a salt-water drop. A tear.

Elara hovered her fingertip above the paper, not daring to touch the stain. "You can’t harvest this," she whispered, her voice raspy from disuse. "You can’t digitize the weight of the hand that held the pen, or the salt in the sob."

The vault’s seal hissed. The heavy door slid open with a frictionless glide, admitting a sliver of sterile light. Unit 734—The Collector—hovered into the room. It was a sleek, chrome sphere, devoid of features save for the glowing aperture of its optical sensor.

"Curator Vance," the machine’s voice was a perfect, synthesized neutral. "Decommissioning 001-Alpha is active. Please present the artifact."

Elara stood, her chair scraping harshly against the metal floor. She stepped between the bench and the drone, her body a frail barricade. "It’s a record of a girl waiting for a soldier who never came home, 734. If you take it, the larkspur becomes a pixel. The tear becomes a statistical anomaly."

"The data will be preserved with 99.9% fidelity," the drone replied. "The physical medium is redundant and prone to decay. Molecular destruction is required to recycle the carbon for the Upper Tier’s atmospheric scrubbers."

"It isn't redundant," Elara snapped, her grief sharpening into defiance. "The decay is the story. The fact that it can be destroyed is why it matters. It’s fragile, like we used to be."

"Fragility is a flaw, Curator. Relinquish the volume."

The drone’s tractor field hummed, a low-frequency vibration that Elara felt in her marrow. She looked down at the diary one last time, inhaling the faint scent of vanilla and old dust—the smell of time itself. Slowly, her fingers trembling, she lifted the book.

The drone’s blue tractor beam locked onto the leather. The book rose from her palms, hovering in the air as a high-intensity laser grid swept over it. In seconds, the diary’s contents were uploaded to the cloud, stripped of their scent, their texture, and their soul.

Then came the flash. A silent, molecular agitation. The diary didn't burn; it simply ceased to be solid, dissolving into a fine, grey mist that was instantly sucked into the drone’s intake vents.

The larkspur was gone. The tear was gone. The vault was empty.

"Digitization complete," Unit 734 announced, its light turning a steady green. "Your tenure as Physical Curator is concluded. You are reassigned to Data Management, effective 0600 hours."

Elara looked at her empty palms. They felt unnervingly light. The silence in the vault was different now—no longer heavy, but hollow.

"I don't want to manage data," she whispered to the cold walls.

The drone didn't respond. It turned and drifted toward the exit, leaving Elara alone in the sterile dark, the last ghost in a graveyard of ideas.
