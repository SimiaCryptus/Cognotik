**Scenario:** A repeated Cournot competition between two firms in a duopoly market.

**Game Type:** non-cooperative

## Game Structure Analysis

This analysis explores the strategic dimensions of a **Repeated Cournot Duopoly**, a foundational model in game theory used to understand how firms interact over time in a market with shared influence over price.

---

### 1. Identify the Game Structure
*   **Game Type:** 
    *   **Non-cooperative:** Each firm makes decisions independently to maximize its own profit, even if their actions result in a "cooperative-like" outcome (tacit collusion).
    *   **Non-zero-sum:** The total profit in the market is not fixed; it depends on the total quantity produced. Both firms can be better off or worse off depending on their mutual choices.
    *   **Simultaneous (within stages):** In each period, firms choose their production quantities at the same time without knowing the other's choice for that specific period.
*   **Temporal Dimension:** 
    *   **Repeated Game:** This is a "supergame" consisting of a stage game (the Cournot interaction) played over multiple periods ($T = 1, 2, \dots, \infty$).
    *   **Horizon:** Usually analyzed as an infinitely repeated game or a game with an uncertain end date to allow for the possibility of stable cooperation.
*   **Information Structure:** 
    *   **Imperfect Information (Stage):** Within a single round, firms do not know the opponent's current move.
    *   **Perfect Information (History):** Firms have "perfect recall," meaning they observe the actual quantities produced by the competitor in all previous rounds.
*   **Asymmetries:** 
    *   Standard models assume **symmetry** (identical marginal costs and identical products), though asymmetries in production capacity or cost structures can be introduced.

### 2. Define Strategy Spaces
*   **Available Strategies:**
    *   **Stage-Game Strategy:** The choice of a quantity $q_i \in [0, \infty)$.
    *   **Repeated-Game Strategy (Meta-strategy):** A complete plan of action that determines $q_i$ in period $t$ based on the history of play in periods $1$ through $t-1$. Examples include:
        *   **Trigger Strategies (e.g., Grim Trigger):** Start by producing the "collusive" quantity; if the opponent cheats, revert to the Nash equilibrium quantity forever.
        *   **Tit-for-Tat:** Mimic the opponent’s previous move.
        *   **Nash Reversion:** A temporary punishment for deviations.
*   **Nature of Strategies:** 
    *   **Continuous:** Quantity is typically treated as a continuous variable.
*   **Constraints:** 
    *   Quantities must be non-negative ($q_i \ge 0$).
    *   Physical capacity constraints may limit the maximum $q_i$ a firm can produce.

### 3. Characterize Payoffs
*   **Objectives:** Each firm seeks to maximize the **Present Value (PV)** of its stream of profits over time.
*   **Payoff Function:** 
    *   The stage payoff is $\pi_i(q_i, q_j) = [P(q_i + q_j) - c_i] \cdot q_i$, where $P$ is the inverse demand function and $c_i$ is the marginal cost.
    *   The total payoff is $V_i = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \delta^t \pi_{it}$, where $\delta \in (0, 1)$ is the **discount factor** (representing the patience of the firm or the probability the game continues).
*   **Interdependence:** Payoffs are highly sensitive to the opponent's choice. If Firm B increases $q_j$, the market price $P$ falls, directly reducing Firm A’s profit.
*   **Transferability:** Payoffs are generally **non-transferable**. Firms cannot legally share profits (side payments) due to anti-cartel laws, making coordination reliant solely on production choices.

### 4. Identify Key Features
*   **The "Shadow of the Future":** The most critical feature. Because the game is repeated, the threat of future punishment can incentivize firms to restrict output today to maintain high prices (Tacit Collusion).
*   **Commitment and Signaling:** 
    *   Current production levels serve as signals. A firm might "signal" a desire to collude by producing a low quantity.
    *   There is no formal mechanism for binding commitment; "contracts" are self-enforcing through the threat of lost future profits.
*   **Communication and Coordination:** 
    *   Explicit communication is usually assumed to be absent (to reflect antitrust realities). 
    *   Coordination is achieved through **Focal Points** or observed historical behavior.
*   **The Folk Theorem:** In this repeated setting, if the discount factor $\delta$ is high enough (i.e., firms are patient), any outcome that is better for both firms than the stage-game Nash equilibrium can be sustained as a Subgame Perfect Equilibrium.
*   **Timing of Moves:** 
    *   **Simultaneous Stage Moves:** Prevents one firm from reacting to the other within the same period.
    *   **Sequential History:** Allows for "punishment phases" in subsequent periods.

## Payoff Matrix

This analysis explores a repeated Cournot competition between two firms. In a Cournot model, firms compete on **quantity** rather than price. Because the game is **repeated**, the firms must consider not only current profits but also the future consequences of their actions (e.g., retaliation).

---

### Part 1: Game Structure Analysis

#### 1. Identify the Game Structure
*   **Type:** Non-cooperative (firms act in their own interest), Simultaneous (in each stage), and Symmetric (assuming identical cost structures).
*   **Repetition:** Repeated game (infinitely repeated or finite with an uncertain end). This allows for the "Folk Theorem" to apply, where cooperation can be sustained through threats of future punishment.
*   **Information:** Imperfect information within each round (moves are simultaneous), but perfect monitoring of past moves (firms see what the other produced in previous rounds).
*   **Asymmetries:** Usually modeled as symmetric (same marginal costs), though asymmetries in production capacity or brand loyalty can exist.

#### 2. Define Strategy Spaces
*   **Available Strategies:** In a discrete representation, firms choose between:
    *   **Collude ($Q_C$):** Producing the low quantity that maximizes joint industry profits (Monopoly level).
    *   **Nash ($Q_N$):** Producing the standard Cournot-Nash equilibrium quantity.
    *   **Defect ($Q_D$):** Producing a high quantity to "cheat" on a collusive agreement and capture more market share.
*   **Nature:** In reality, the strategy space is **continuous** ($q \in [0, \infty)$), but for strategic analysis, we focus on these discrete benchmarks.
*   **Constraints:** Production is limited by capacity and the fact that quantity cannot be negative.

#### 3. Characterize Payoffs
*   **Objectives:** Maximization of the present value of the stream of profits over time.
*   **Dependence:** Payoffs are highly interdependent. If Firm A increases quantity, the market price drops for both firms, reducing Firm B's profit margin.
*   **Transferability:** Payoffs (profits) are generally non-transferable unless there is an explicit (and often illegal) side-payment agreement.

#### 4. Key Features
*   **Commitment/Signaling:** Firms can use "Trigger Strategies" (like the Grim Trigger or Tit-for-Tat) to signal that they will punish defection in future rounds.
*   **Timing:** Moves are simultaneous in each period, but the sequence of periods creates a dynamic interaction.
*   **Incentives:** There is a constant tension between the short-term incentive to cheat (produce more) and the long-term benefit of maintaining high prices through collusion.

*   **Payoffs:** (1012, 1012). This is the highest joint payoff.
*   **Explanation:** By acting like a shared monopoly, they maximize industry profit. However, this is unstable in a one-shot game because both have an incentive to cheat.

*   **Outcome:** Both firms produce the quantity where their best-response functions intersect.
*   **Payoffs:** (900, 900).

### Strategic Implications for the Repeated Game

In a **repeated** context, the matrix above is just one "slice" of time. The firms' strategies evolve:

1.  **Sustainability of Collusion:** If the firms value future profits highly (high discount factor $\delta$), they will choose **(Collude, Collude)**. They know that if they play **Defect** today to get 1139, the other firm will play **Nash** or **Defect** forever after, trapping them in a 900 or 810 payoff cycle.
2.  **Punishment Mechanisms:**
    *   **Grim Trigger:** If you defect once, I will play "Nash" forever.
    *   **Tit-for-Tat:** I will do today what you did in the previous round.
3.  **The "Folk Theorem" Conclusion:** In this repeated Cournot game, any payoff between the Nash equilibrium (900) and the Collusive optimum (1012) is achievable as a Subgame Perfect Equilibrium, provided the firms are sufficiently patient.

## Nash Equilibria Analysis

This analysis explores a repeated Cournot competition between two firms (Firm A and Firm B) in a duopoly. In a Cournot model, firms compete on **quantity** rather than price. Because the game is **repeated**, the strategic landscape shifts from a simple one-shot interaction to a complex dynamic where reputation, punishment, and cooperation become possible.

---

### Part 1: Game Structure Analysis

**1. Game Type:**
*   **Non-cooperative:** There are no binding contracts; firms choose quantities independently to maximize their own profit.
*   **Repeated:** This is a "supergame" consisting of the same stage game played over multiple periods ($t = 1, 2, \dots, T$ or $\infty$).
*   **Simultaneous (within stages):** In each period, firms choose their quantities $q_A$ and $q_B$ without knowing the other's choice for that specific period.
*   **Information:** Imperfect information within the stage (simultaneous moves), but typically **perfect monitoring** (each firm observes the other’s past quantity choices).

**2. Strategy Spaces:**
*   **Continuous:** Strategies are quantities $q_i \in [0, Q_{max}]$.
*   **Repeated Game Strategies:** A strategy is a complete plan of action specifying the quantity to produce in period $t$ based on the entire history of play up to $t-1$.

**3. Payoffs:**
*   **Objective:** Maximize the present value of the stream of profits: $V_i = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \delta^t \pi_{it}$, where $\delta \in (0,1)$ is the discount factor (representing the "patience" of the firm or the probability the game continues).
*   **Stage Profit:** $\pi_i(q_i, q_j) = [P(q_i + q_j) - c]q_i$, where $P$ is the market price and $c$ is the marginal cost.

**4. Key Features:**
*   **Timing:** Moves are simultaneous in each period.
*   **Incentives:** There is a constant tension between the collective benefit of restricting output (collusion) and the individual incentive to increase output (cheating).

---

### Part 2: Nash Equilibrium Analysis

In a repeated Cournot game, there are two primary types of Nash Equilibria (specifically Subgame Perfect Nash Equilibria, or SPNE).

#### Equilibrium 1: The Stage-Game Nash (The "Non-Cooperative" Path)
1.  **Strategy Profile:** Both firms produce the Cournot quantity $q_C = \frac{a-c}{3b}$ in every period, regardless of history.
2.  **Why it is a Nash Equilibrium:** If Firm B always produces $q_C$, Firm A’s best response is always $q_C$. Neither firm can improve its total discounted payoff by changing its quantity in any period if the other remains at $q_C$.
3.  **Classification:** Pure Strategy Equilibrium.
4.  **Stability and Likelihood:** **Highly Stable.** This is the "default" outcome. It does not require trust or a high discount factor ($\delta$). It is the likely outcome if firms are short-sighted or if the market is highly unstable.

#### Equilibrium 2: The Collusive Equilibrium (The "Trigger Strategy" Path)
1.  **Strategy Profile (Grim Trigger):** 
    *   **Period 1:** Produce the monopoly share $q_M/2 = \frac{a-c}{4b}$.
    *   **Period $t$:** Produce $q_M/2$ if both firms have produced $q_M/2$ in all previous periods. If anyone has ever deviated, produce $q_C$ forever (the "trigger").
2.  **Why it is a Nash Equilibrium:** This is an equilibrium if the **Discount Factor ($\delta$)** is high enough. A firm will not deviate if:
    *   *(Gain from cheating today)* $<$ *(Loss of future cooperative profits)*.
    *   In a standard linear Cournot model, this equilibrium holds if $\delta \geq 9/17 \approx 0.53$.
3.  **Classification:** Pure Strategy Equilibrium (in the supergame).
4.  **Stability and Likelihood:** **Conditionally Stable.** It depends entirely on the firms' patience ($\delta$) and the credibility of the threat. If $\delta$ is low, this equilibrium collapses.

---

### Part 3: Discussion of Multiple Equilibria

The **Folk Theorem** suggests that in infinitely repeated games, there are infinitely many Nash equilibria (any payoff between the Cournot profit and the Monopoly profit can be sustained if $\delta$ is high enough).

**1. Which is most likely to occur?**
*   **The Stage-Game NE ($q_C$)** is most likely if the firms are in a "one-off" mindset, if the legal environment strictly forbids any signaling, or if the discount factor is low (e.g., high interest rates or high probability of market exit).
*   **The Collusive Equilibrium** is more likely in mature, stable industries with few players (duopoly) where firms "learn" to coordinate over time to avoid mutually destructive overproduction.

**2. Coordination Problems:**
*   **The "Focal Point" Problem:** Even if both firms want to cooperate, they may disagree on the "fair" quantity. If Firm A has lower costs than Firm B, they will disagree on what the "collusive" quantity should be.
*   **Signal Noise:** If a firm sees the market price drop, it might not know if the other firm cheated (increased $q$) or if market demand simply fell. This can trigger "price wars" (punishment phases) by mistake.

**3. Pareto Dominance:**
*   The **Collusive Equilibrium Pareto-dominates** the Stage-Game Nash Equilibrium. Both firms earn strictly higher profits under collusion ($\pi_M/2$) than under Cournot competition ($\pi_C$).
*   However, the Collusive Equilibrium is **riskier**. If Firm A attempts to collude and Firm B cheats, Firm A suffers a significant loss in Period 1. This "Risk Dominance" often leads firms back to the less-profitable but safer Cournot-Nash equilibrium.

 **Requirement** | None | High Discount Factor ($\delta$) |
 **Trust Needed** | Low | High |

## Dominant Strategies Analysis

This analysis examines a **Repeated Cournot Duopoly**, where two firms (A and B) compete by choosing quantities ($q_A, q_B$) simultaneously in each period, with the market price determined by the total supply.

---

### Part 1: Game Structure Analysis

1.  **Game Type**: 
    *   **Non-cooperative**: Firms act in their own self-interest; any "cooperation" (collusion) must be self-enforcing.
    *   **Repeated**: The stage game (Cournot competition) is played multiple times (potentially infinitely).
    *   **Simultaneous (within stages)**: In each period, firms choose quantities without knowing the other's choice for that period.
    *   **Continuous**: Strategy spaces are typically continuous (any quantity $q \ge 0$).

2.  **Strategy Spaces**:
    *   **Stage Game**: A continuous range of quantities $[0, Q_{max}]$.
    *   **Repeated Game**: "Meta-strategies" or "Trigger strategies." A player’s choice in period $t$ is a function of the history of play in periods $1$ through $t-1$. Examples include **Grim Trigger**, **Tit-for-Tat**, or **Always Cournot**.

3.  **Payoffs**:
    *   **Objective**: Maximize the present value of the sum of profits over time: $\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \delta^t \pi_{it}$, where $\delta$ is the discount factor (patience).
    *   **Interdependence**: Firm A’s profit decreases as Firm B’s quantity increases because it drives down the market price.

4.  **Key Features**:
    *   **Information**: Perfect monitoring (firms observe each other's past quantities) but imperfect information within the stage (simultaneous moves).
    *   **Timing**: Infinite repetition allows for the "Folk Theorem," suggesting that if firms are patient enough, they can sustain collusion (monopoly profits) through the threat of future punishment.

---

### Part 2: Dominant Strategy Analysis

In a Cournot competition, the optimal move for one firm depends entirely on the move of the other. This creates a state of **strategic interdependence**.

#### 1. Strictly Dominant Strategies
*   **None.**
*   In Cournot competition, there is **no strictly dominant strategy**. If Firm B produces a very high quantity, Firm A’s best response is to produce a small quantity to avoid crashing the price. If Firm B produces a very low quantity, Firm A’s best response is to produce a large quantity to capture the market. Because the "best" move changes based on the opponent's action, no single strategy is best in all circumstances.

#### 2. Weakly Dominant Strategies
*   **None.**
*   For the same reasons as above, no quantity choice is "at least as good" as all others regardless of the opponent's choice. Every quantity $q_i$ has a specific scenario (a specific $q_j$) where it is the optimal response, and other scenarios where it is suboptimal.

#### 3. Dominated Strategies
While there are no dominant strategies, there are many **dominated strategies** (strategies that are never a best response):
*   **Quantities exceeding the Monopoly Quantity ($q_i > q_m$):** While not always strictly dominated in the mathematical sense against *all* possible moves, producing more than the total monopoly quantity is generally considered irrational as it drives prices toward marginal cost regardless of the opponent.
*   **Quantities exceeding the "Perfect Competition" Quantity ($q_i > q_c$):** Any quantity that results in a market price below marginal cost ($P < MC$) is strictly dominated by producing $q=0$. Producing at a loss is always worse than producing nothing.
*   **In the Repeated Context:** "Always Collude" (producing the monopoly share regardless of being cheated on) is dominated by "Trigger Strategies" because it fails to protect the firm's long-term interests against a defector.

#### 4. Iteratively Eliminated Strategies
Through the process of **Iterated Deletion of Strictly Dominated Strategies (IDSDS)**, we can narrow the strategy space:
1.  **Step 1**: Eliminate any $q_i$ that is higher than the quantity a firm would produce if it were a monopolist ($q_{max} = (a-c)/b$). No rational firm would produce more than the total market demand at $P=MC$.
2.  **Step 2**: Knowing the opponent will not produce more than the monopoly quantity, Firm A can eliminate very low quantities that would only be optimal if the opponent were producing an impossibly high amount.
3.  **The Result**: In a one-shot Cournot game, this iterative process converges on the **Nash Equilibrium quantity**. Any strategy other than the Nash Equilibrium quantity is eventually "eliminated" as a non-best response to the remaining rational choices of the opponent.

---

### Strategic Implications

1.  **The "Incentive to Cheat"**: Because there is no dominant strategy to "Cooperate," firms face a Prisoner's Dilemma in every round. The Nash Equilibrium (the result of iterative elimination) leads to higher production and lower profits than a monopoly would.
2.  **The Role of Punishment**: In a repeated game, the absence of a dominant strategy allows for **conditional cooperation**. Firms use "Trigger Strategies" (like Grim Trigger) to make cooperation the rational choice. The "threat" of reverting to the Cournot-Nash quantity (the result of the IDSDS) serves as the punishment that sustains collusion.
3.  **Stability**: Because the Cournot-Nash equilibrium is reached by eliminating dominated strategies, it is a stable point. Neither firm has an incentive to deviate unilaterally once they are both playing the Nash quantity.
4.  **Market Outcome**: The lack of a dominant strategy to restrict output means that without the "shadow of the future" (repeated play), duopoly markets will always result in lower prices and higher quantities than a monopoly, benefiting consumers at the expense of firm profits.

## Pareto Optimality Analysis

This analysis examines a **Repeated Cournot Competition** between two firms (Firm A and Firm B). In this model, firms compete on the quantity of a homogeneous product produced, and the market price is determined by the total supply.

---

### Part 1: Game Structure Analysis

**1. Game Type**
*   **Non-Cooperative:** There is no binding, enforceable agreement between the firms.
*   **Repeated Game:** This is an infinitely repeated game (or a game with an uncertain end date). This allows for "supergame" strategies where current actions influence future payoffs.
*   **Simultaneous Moves:** In each period, firms choose their quantities ($q_A, q_B$) without knowing the other's choice for that specific period.
*   **Information:** Generally assumed to be **Perfect and Complete** regarding the past (firms observe previous quantities) but **Imperfect** within the period (simultaneous moves).

**2. Strategy Spaces**
*   **Continuous Strategies:** Each firm chooses a quantity $q_i \in [0, \infty)$.
*   **Repeated Game Strategies:** Strategies are not just single numbers but "plans of action" (e.g., Grim Trigger, Tit-for-Tat) that dictate quantity choices based on the history of the game.

**3. Characterize Payoffs**
*   **Objective:** Maximize the present value of the stream of profits: $V_i = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \delta^t \pi_{i,t}$, where $\delta$ is the discount factor.
*   **Profit Function:** $\pi_i(q_i, q_j) = q_i \cdot [P(q_i + q_j) - c]$, where $P$ is the inverse demand function and $c$ is the marginal cost.
*   **Non-Transferable:** Payoffs are usually internal profits, though side payments are theoretically possible but often illegal (antitrust).

**4. Key Features**
*   **Timing:** Discrete time periods $t = 1, 2, 3...$
*   **Signaling:** Firms can use "quantity signaling" to indicate a willingness to cooperate or to punish "cheating."
*   **The Folk Theorem:** In repeated games, if the discount factor $\delta$ is high enough, any payoff that is better for both players than the minimax (Nash) payoff can be sustained as an equilibrium.

---

### Part 2: Pareto Optimality Analysis

In the context of a duopoly, **Pareto optimality** is analyzed from the perspective of the **firms' profits** (Producer Pareto Optimality).

#### 1. Identification of Pareto Optimal Outcomes
The Pareto optimal outcomes for the firms lie on the **Contract Curve**, which represents all combinations of $(q_A, q_B)$ that maximize the **total industry profit** ($\Pi = \pi_A + \pi_B$).
*   **The Monopoly Outcome:** The most prominent Pareto optimal outcome is where $q_A + q_B = Q_m$ (the quantity a single monopolist would produce).
*   **The Frontier:** Any point where $q_A + q_B = Q_m$ is Pareto optimal for the firms. If Firm A produces more, Firm B must produce less to keep the price high enough to maximize total profit.

#### 2. Comparison: Pareto Optimal vs. Nash Equilibrium
*   **Cournot-Nash Equilibrium ($q_c$):** In a one-shot game, firms produce where their best-response functions intersect. This results in a total quantity $Q_c$ that is **higher** than the monopoly quantity ($Q_c > Q_m$).
*   **The Gap:** Because $Q_c > Q_m$, the market price in a Nash equilibrium is lower than the monopoly price. Consequently, the total profit at the Nash equilibrium is strictly lower than the total profit at any Pareto optimal point.
*   **Conclusion:** The Cournot-Nash equilibrium is **not Pareto optimal** for the firms.

#### 3. Pareto Improvements over Equilibrium Outcomes
A Pareto improvement occurs if we move from the Cournot-Nash quantities toward the monopoly frontier.
*   **The Improvement:** If both firms agree to reduce their output from $q_c$ to $q_m/2$ (half the monopoly quantity), both firms see an increase in profit.
*   **Direction of Shift:** Any reduction in total quantity that moves the industry closer to $Q_m$ while maintaining a distribution of profit where neither firm earns less than their Cournot profit is a Pareto improvement.

#### 4. Efficiency vs. Equilibrium Trade-offs
*   **The Incentive to Cheat:** While the Pareto optimal outcome (collusion) yields higher profits, it is not a Nash equilibrium in a one-shot game. If Firm B produces the "cooperative" quantity, Firm A’s best response is to "cheat" by producing a higher quantity to capture more market share at a high price.
*   **The Repeated Game Solution:** In the repeated version, the trade-off is managed via **Trigger Strategies**.
    *   **Short-term Gain:** Cheating provides a one-time profit boost.
    *   **Long-term Loss:** Cheating triggers a "price war" (returning to Cournot-Nash or lower), leading to lower future profits.
*   **The Efficiency Trade-off:** From a **social** perspective (including consumers), the Cournot-Nash equilibrium is actually "more efficient" than the Pareto optimal (monopoly) outcome because it results in lower prices and less deadweight loss. However, from the **firms'** perspective, the Cournot-Nash equilibrium represents a coordination failure.

### Opportunities for Cooperation/Coordination
To reach the Pareto optimal outcome in a repeated Cournot game, firms use:
1.  **Tacit Collusion:** Using "Grim Trigger" strategies where firms produce $Q_m/2$ as long as the other does, but revert to $q_c$ forever if anyone cheats.
2.  **Price Leadership:** One firm sets a "standard" quantity, and the other follows to signal stability.
3.  **Communication (Cheap Talk):** While explicit price-fixing is illegal, public announcements about "capacity constraints" or "profitability targets" can serve as coordination signals to move toward the Pareto frontier.

## Repeated Game Analysis

This analysis explores a **Repeated Cournot Duopoly** specifically constrained to **5 iterations**. In a Cournot model, firms compete on quantity ($Q$), and the market price is determined by the aggregate supply.

---

### 1. Game Structure Analysis

*   **Game Type:** Non-cooperative, repeated, simultaneous-move stage game.
*   **Players:** Firm A and Firm B (Duopolists).
*   **Strategy Space:** Continuous. Each firm chooses a quantity $q_i \in [0, \infty)$.
    *   *Key Benchmarks:* $q_C$ (Competitive/Bertrand), $q_{NE}$ (Cournot-Nash Equilibrium), and $q_M$ (Monopoly/Collusive quantity).
*   **Payoffs:** Profits $\pi_i(q_i, q_j) = (P(q_i + q_j) - c)q_i$. Payoffs are non-transferable.
*   **Information:** Perfect and complete. Players observe the quantities chosen by the opponent in all previous rounds before the next round begins.

---

### 2. Finite vs. Infinite Horizon: The 5-Round Constraint
The most critical factor in this analysis is the **Finite Horizon (T=5)**. 

*   **Backward Induction:** According to Selten’s Theorem, if a stage game has a unique Nash Equilibrium (which Cournot does), any finitely repeated version of that game has a unique Subgame Perfect Equilibrium: **playing the stage-game Nash Equilibrium in every round.**
*   **The Unraveling Effect:** 
    *   In **Round 5**, there is no future to consider. It is a one-shot game. Both firms will play $q_{NE}$ to maximize immediate profit.
    *   In **Round 4**, players know that regardless of what they do, Round 5 will result in $q_{NE}$. Therefore, there is no "threat" or "reward" available in the future to incentivize cooperation in Round 4. They play $q_{NE}$.
    *   This logic regresses to **Round 1**.

---

### 3. Folk Theorem Application
In an **infinitely** repeated game, the Folk Theorem suggests that any payoff vector that is individually rational and feasible can be sustained as an equilibrium if the discount factor is high enough.

*   **In this 5-round game:** The standard Folk Theorem **does not apply** strictly because the game is finite and has a unique stage-game NE. 
*   **Modified Expectation:** However, if we introduce **bounded rationality** or **incomplete information** (e.g., Firm A isn't sure if Firm B is a "tit-for-tat" bot), "Folk-like" outcomes (collusion) can be sustained for the first 3 or 4 rounds before the inevitable "end-game" defection.

---

### 4. Trigger Strategies and Punishment
Trigger strategies are used to enforce the collusive quantity ($q_m = q_M/2$), which yields higher profits than $q_{NE}$.

*   **Grim Trigger:** "I will produce $q_m$ as long as you do. If you ever deviate, I will produce $q_{NE}$ (or even the competitive quantity $q_C$) for all remaining rounds."
*   **Effectiveness in T=5:** Weak. The "punishment" duration is too short. If Firm A cheats in Round 3, Firm B can only punish them in Rounds 4 and 5. The one-time gain from cheating in Round 3 often outweighs the lost profits in Rounds 4 and 5, especially as the game nears its end.

---

### 5. Reputation Effects
In a short 5-round window, reputation is a tool to delay the "unraveling" of cooperation.

*   **Signaling:** Firm A might produce the collusive quantity in Round 1 specifically to signal that it is a "cooperative type." 
*   **The "Crazy" Strategy:** If Firm A acts as if it will *only* play a trigger strategy (ignoring backward induction), Firm B may find it more profitable to cooperate for the first few rounds rather than triggering an immediate price war.
*   **Value:** Reputation is most valuable in Rounds 1–3. By Round 4, reputation is usually discarded for a "final grab" of profit.

---

### 6. Discount Factors ($\delta$)
The discount factor $\delta \in [0, 1]$ represents the value of tomorrow’s profit today.

*   **High $\delta$ (near 1):** Players value future rounds highly. Even in a 5-round game, a high $\delta$ makes the threat of punishment in Rounds 4 and 5 more meaningful, potentially sustaining cooperation until Round 3.
*   **Low $\delta$ (near 0):** Players are "short-sighted." They will treat every round like a one-shot game, leading to immediate Cournot-Nash levels ($q_{NE}$) from Round 1.

---

### 7. Strategic Recommendations

Given the 5-iteration constraint, firms should adopt a **"Modified Tit-for-Tat"** approach rather than a strict Nash strategy or a naive Collusive strategy.

#### **Recommendation for Firm A:**
1.  **Rounds 1–2 (Cooperation Phase):** Start by producing the collusive quantity ($q_m$). This signals a desire for high mutual profits. The potential gains from cooperation in these early rounds are worth the risk of being "cheated" on once.
2.  **Round 3 (Observation Phase):** If Firm B cooperated in Rounds 1 and 2, continue to cooperate. If Firm B deviated, immediately switch to $q_{NE}$ (Grim Trigger).
3.  **Round 4 (The Pre-emptive Strike):** This is the "danger zone." Most rational players will prepare to defect here. If you suspect Firm B is highly rational, you should consider increasing your quantity toward $q_{NE}$ to avoid being the "sucker" when they inevitably defect.
4.  **Round 5 (The Final Round):** **Always play the Cournot-Nash quantity ($q_{NE}$).** There is zero strategic benefit to cooperation in the final round, as there is no future to influence.

#### **Summary Table of Strategy**
 Round | Strategy | Logic |
 :--- | :--- | :--- |
 **1** | Collusive ($q_m$) | Establish reputation and signal intent. |
 **2** | Collusive ($q_m$) | Maintain high-profit equilibrium. |
 **3** | Tit-for-Tat | Reward cooperation or punish early defection. |
 **4** | Transition to $q_{NE}$ | Protect against the "end-game" unraveling. |
 **5** | Cournot-Nash ($q_{NE}$) | Pure profit maximization; no future consequences. |

## Strategic Recommendations

This analysis provides strategic recommendations for **Firm A** and **Firm B** in a repeated Cournot duopoly. In this game, firms compete on the quantity produced, and the market price is determined by the total aggregate supply. Because the game is **repeated**, the firms move beyond the one-shot Nash Equilibrium toward potential "tacit collusion."

---

### Strategic Recommendations for Firm A & Firm B

Since the firms are in a symmetric duopoly, the strategic logic applies equally to both.

#### 1. Optimal Strategy: Tacit Collusion (The "Monopoly-Half" Strategy)
*   **The Strategy**: Each firm should produce exactly half of the total monopoly quantity ($q_m/2$).
*   **Why**: In a one-shot Cournot game, the Nash Equilibrium leads to higher production and lower profits than a monopoly would achieve. In a repeated game, firms can achieve higher "Joint Profit Maximization." By restricting output, both firms maintain a higher market price, resulting in higher discounted lifetime utility than if they constantly fought for market share.

#### 2. Contingent Strategies: The "Grim Trigger" or "Tit-for-Tat"
*   **Grim Trigger**: If the opponent deviates (produces more than the agreed/tacit quantity), you must respond by producing the one-shot Nash Equilibrium quantity forever. This serves as a maximum deterrent.
*   **Tit-for-Tat (More Robust)**: If the opponent cheats in period $t$, you cheat in period $t+1$, but return to cooperation if they return to cooperation. This is less "brittle" than Grim Trigger and accounts for potential miscalculations or temporary market shocks.
*   **Recommendation**: Use a **"Trigger Strategy with a Grace Period."** If the opponent overproduces slightly, signal a warning. If they overproduce significantly, revert to Nash quantities to punish them until they signal a return to the collusive level.

#### 3. Risk Assessment
*   **The "Cheating" Incentive**: There is a constant short-term temptation to "cheat" by producing a bit more to capture a larger slice of the high-price pie.
*   **Discount Factor Risk**: If your opponent is cash-strapped or has a "short horizon" (low $\delta$), they value today’s profit more than future cooperation. They are highly likely to defect.
*   **Demand Volatility**: If market demand drops, the price will fall. You might mistake a market downturn for your opponent "cheating" by overproducing, leading to an accidental and destructive price/quantity war.

#### 4. Coordination Opportunities (Tacit)
*   **Price Leadership**: One firm can act as a "barometer," adjusting quantities first to signal a move toward a more profitable equilibrium.
*   **Public Announcements**: While explicit price-fixing is illegal (Antitrust/Competition Law), firms can use public earnings calls to signal a focus on "margin over volume" or "capacity discipline." This signals to the competitor that you do not intend to flood the market.

#### 5. Information Considerations
*   **Monitoring**: Invest in market intelligence to accurately estimate the opponent's production levels. Since you only see the resulting market price, you must be able to distinguish between a price drop caused by the opponent's overproduction versus a drop in consumer demand.
*   **Transparency**: Sometimes, being transparent about your own capacity constraints can actually *help* cooperation, as it signals to the opponent that you *cannot* easily cheat even if you wanted to.

---

### Overall Strategic Insights
*   **The Shadow of the Future**: The success of a repeated Cournot game depends entirely on the "Shadow of the Future." If firms believe they will compete for a long time, cooperation is stable. If the industry is dying or a firm is facing bankruptcy, expect a "race to the bottom" in production.
*   **Credibility is Currency**: Your threat to punish a defector must be credible. If you are known to be "soft" and don't punish overproduction, your opponent will continually erode your market share.

### Potential Pitfalls
*   **The Finite Game Trap**: If both firms know the competition will end at a specific date (e.g., a patent expiry), the cooperation will likely collapse in the final periods due to backward induction.
*   **Over-Aggression**: Trying to "win" the market share battle often results in a "War of Attrition" where both firms lose more money than a monopolist would ever make.
*   **Legal/Regulatory Risk**: Be wary of "Tacit Collusion" becoming too obvious. Regulators monitor markets where prices remain artificially high despite low demand, which can trigger anti-cartel investigations.

---

### Implementation Guidance: How to Execute

1.  **Establish the "Status Quo"**: Start by producing at a stable, moderate level. Avoid sudden spikes in quantity that could be interpreted as an aggressive move.
2.  **Monitor the Price Signal**: If the market price drops unexpectedly, analyze whether it is a demand shift. If you suspect the opponent has increased quantity, implement a **proportional response** (increase your quantity slightly) to signal that you will not cede share.
3.  **Maintain Capacity Discipline**: Do not build massive excess production capacity. Having the *ability* to flood the market is a threat, but actually having the inventory forces you to sell it, which signals aggression to your opponent.
4.  **Communicate via Action**: In Cournot competition, your "moves" are your quantities. Use your production schedule as a language. Consistent, predictable output levels foster a stable, profitable duopoly.
