## Game Structure Analysis
<details><summary>Full Analysis</summary>

This analysis examines a **Repeated Cournot Duopoly**, a foundational model in industrial organization and game theory that explores how firms compete on quantity over time.

---

### 1. Identify the Game Structure
*   **Game Type:** 
    *   **Non-cooperative:** Each firm makes decisions independently to maximize its own profit, even if their actions affect one another.
    *   **Non-zero-sum:** The total profit in the market is not fixed; both firms can be profitable, or both can suffer losses (e.g., through overproduction).
*   **Temporal Nature:** 
    *   **Repeated Game:** This is not a one-shot interaction. The stage game (Cournot competition) is played multiple times (either infinitely or with an uncertain end date). This allows for the evolution of "supergame" strategies.
*   **Information Structure:**
    *   **Imperfect Information (within the stage):** In each period, firms choose their quantities simultaneously without knowing the other's choice for that specific period.
    *   **Perfect Information (historical):** Players have "perfect recall," meaning they can observe the past quantity choices of their competitor before making a move in the next period.
*   **Asymmetries:** 
    *   Standard models often assume **symmetry** (identical marginal costs and discount factors), but asymmetries can exist if one firm has a lower cost structure or a different valuation of future profits (discount factor $\delta$).

### 2. Define Strategy Spaces
*   **Available Strategies:**
    *   In the **stage game**, the strategy is the choice of quantity $q_i$.
    *   In the **repeated game**, a strategy is a complete contingent plan that specifies the quantity to produce in period $t$ based on the entire history of play from periods $1$ to $t-1$.
*   **Discrete vs. Continuous:** 
    *   Strategies are typically **continuous**. Firms can choose any quantity $q_i \in [0, Q_{max}]$, where $Q_{max}$ is the quantity at which the market price hits zero.
*   **Constraints:**
    *   **Non-negativity:** $q_i \geq 0$.
    *   **Capacity:** Firms may be limited by physical production capacity ($q_i \leq K_i$).
    *   **Interdependence:** The strategy of one firm is constrained by the "Reaction Function" of the other; the optimal $q_A$ depends on the expected $q_B$.

### 3. Characterize Payoffs
*   **Objectives:** The primary objective is the maximization of the **Present Value (PV)** of the stream of profits over time.
    *   $\Pi_i = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \delta^t \pi_{it}$, where $\delta$ is the discount factor ($0 < \delta < 1$).
*   **Outcome Dependence:**
    *   The profit for Firm A in any period is $\pi_A = (P(q_A + q_B) - c_A) \cdot q_A$.
    *   Payoffs are highly interdependent: if Firm B increases its quantity, the market price $P$ falls, reducing Firm A's profit even if Firm A's production remains constant.
*   **Transferability:** 
    *   Payoffs are generally **non-transferable**. In a non-cooperative duopoly, firms cannot legally engage in side payments (redistributing profits) due to anti-trust regulations.

### 4. Identify Key Features
*   **Commitment and Signaling:**
    *   In a repeated game, current production levels serve as a **signal** of intent. A firm might produce at a "collusive" level (lower quantity) to signal a desire for cooperation.
*   **Coordination (Tacit Collusion):**
    *   While explicit communication is forbidden, firms can achieve **tacit collusion**. They may use "Trigger Strategies" (like the **Grim Trigger**):
        *   *Cooperate:* Produce the monopoly quantity (half each) as long as the other does.
        *   *Punish:* If the opponent cheats, revert to the Nash-Cournot equilibrium quantity forever.
*   **Timing of Moves:**
    *   **Simultaneous within stages:** Neither firm has a "First-Mover Advantage" (unlike the Stackelberg model).
    *   **Sequential across stages:** The repetition creates a sequential feedback loop where today's move influences tomorrow's environment.
*   **The Folk Theorem:**
    *   A key feature of this repeated game is that if the discount factor $\delta$ is sufficiently high (i.e., firms value future profits enough), any payoff that is better than the Nash-Cournot equilibrium can be sustained as a Subgame Perfect Equilibrium.

| Feature | Description |
| :--- | :--- |

| **Equilibrium Concept** | Nash Equilibrium (Stage); Subgame Perfect Equilibrium (Repeated) |

| **Incentive to Cheat** | High (Short-term gain from "dumping" quantity on the market) |

## Payoff Matrix
<details><summary>Full Matrix</summary>

This analysis examines a repeated Cournot competition between two firms (Firm A and Firm B). In this model, firms compete on the **quantity** of a homogeneous product produced, and the market price is determined by the total supply.

---

### Part 1: Game Structure Analysis

#### 1. Identify the Game Structure
*   **Type:** Non-cooperative. While firms may attempt to collude, there is no binding legal enforcement to ensure they stick to an agreement.
*   **Repetition:** Repeated game. This is crucial because it allows for "trigger strategies" (punishing a defector in future rounds), which can sustain cooperation that would be impossible in a one-shot game.
*   **Information:** Imperfect information within each round (simultaneous moves), but perfect information regarding the history of moves (each firm sees what the other produced in previous rounds).
*   **Asymmetries:** Generally modeled as symmetric (identical marginal costs and product quality), though asymmetries in production capacity or costs can exist.

#### 2. Define Strategy Spaces
*   **Available Strategies:** In each period, a firm chooses a quantity $q_i \in [0, \infty)$.
*   **Discrete vs. Continuous:** While quantity is technically continuous, for strategic analysis, we often categorize choices into:
    *   **Collude ($q_C$):** Producing half of the monopoly output.
    *   **Cournot-Nash ($q_N$):** The standard equilibrium where neither firm can improve profit by changing quantity alone.
    *   **Defect/Cheat ($q_D$):** Producing the "Best Response" to the other firm's collusion to capture more market share.
*   **Constraints:** Production is limited by capacity ($q \ge 0$) and the fact that total quantity ($Q$) eventually drives price below marginal cost.

#### 3. Characterize Payoffs
*   **Objectives:** Maximization of the present value of the stream of profits over time.
*   **Dependence:** Payoffs are highly interdependent. If Firm A increases $q_A$, the market price $P$ drops, reducing Firm B’s profit.
*   **Transferability:** Non-transferable. Profits are earned individually based on market sales.

#### 4. Key Features
*   **Timing:** Simultaneous moves within each period.
*   **Signaling:** In a repeated game, a firm might produce a low quantity in period 1 to "signal" a desire to collude.
*   **Discount Factor ($\delta$):** The value firms place on future profits. If $\delta$ is high (firms are patient), collusion is more likely to be stable.

---

### Part 2: Stage-Game Payoff Matrix

To construct a clear matrix, we simplify the continuous quantity choices into three discrete strategic stances. 

**Assumptions for Numerical Values:**
*   **Market Demand:** $P = 100 - (q_A + q_B)$
*   **Marginal Cost:** $c = 10$
*   **Monopoly Output (Collusion):** Total $Q=45$; each firm produces **22.5**.
*   **Cournot-Nash Output:** Each firm produces **30**.
*   **Defection Output:** If one colludes (22.5), the other's best response is **33.75**.

| Firm A \ Firm B | Collude ($q=22.5$) | Compete/Nash ($q=30$) | Defect/Cheat ($q=33.75$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Collude** | (1012, 1012) | (787, 1050) | (759, 1139) |
| **Compete/Nash** | (1050, 787) | (900, 900) | (843, 949) |
| **Defect/Cheat** | (1139, 759) | (949, 843) | (885, 885) |

---

### Part 3: Outcome Explanations

#### 1. Collude / Collude (The Cooperative Outcome)
*   **Payoff:** (1012, 1012)
*   **Explanation:** Both firms restrict output to act like a single monopolist. This results in the highest **joint** profit. However, this is unstable in a one-shot game because both firms have an incentive to "cheat" and produce more to capture more profit.

#### 2. Compete / Compete (The Cournot-Nash Equilibrium)
*   **Payoff:** (900, 900)
*   **Explanation:** This is the standard non-cooperative equilibrium. Neither firm can increase its profit by changing its quantity given the other's choice. It is lower than the collusion payoff but higher than the "Defect/Defect" outcome.

#### 3. Defect / Collude (The "Sucker" Outcome)
*   **Payoff:** (1139, 759)
*   **Explanation:** Firm A "cheats" on the agreement by producing more ($q=33.75$) while Firm B remains faithful to the low-quantity agreement ($q=22.5$). Firm A captures a massive market share at a relatively high price, while Firm B’s profit is cannibalized.

#### 4. Defect / Defect (The Overproduction Trap)
*   **Payoff:** (885, 885)
*   **Explanation:** Both firms attempt to cheat the other simultaneously. The market becomes flooded with goods, causing the price to crash. Both firms end up worse off than if they had simply stayed at the Cournot-Nash level.

---

### Strategic Analysis of the Repeated Game
In the **repeated** version of this game, the firms look at the matrix differently:
1.  **The Temptation:** In any single round, the move from *Collude* to *Defect* gains a firm +127 (1139 - 1012).
2.  **The Punishment:** If Firm B uses a "Grim Trigger" strategy, they will move to *Compete/Nash* forever if Firm A defects. This costs Firm A -112 (1012 - 900) every single period for the rest of time.
3.  **Conclusion:** If the discount factor ($\delta$) is high enough (specifically $\delta \ge 0.53$ in this model), the long-term loss of 112 per period outweighs the one-time gain of 127, and **stable collusion becomes the rational equilibrium.**

## Nash Equilibria Analysis
<details><summary>Full Analysis</summary>

This analysis explores a **Repeated Cournot Competition** between two firms (Firm A and Firm B). In a Cournot model, firms compete on the quantity of output produced, which then determines the market price.

---

### Part 1: Game Structure Analysis

**1. Game Type:**
*   **Non-cooperative:** Each firm maximizes its own profit without a binding, enforceable contract.
*   **Repeated Game:** This is not a one-shot interaction. The firms play the same stage game over multiple periods ($t = 1, 2, \dots, T$ or $\infty$).
*   **Simultaneous (within stages):** In each period, firms choose their quantities simultaneously without knowing the other's choice for that specific period.
*   **Information:** Imperfect information within the stage (simultaneous moves), but typically **perfect monitoring** (each firm observes the other's past quantity choices).

**2. Strategy Spaces:**
*   **Stage Game:** The strategy is the quantity $q_i \in [0, Q_{max}]$. This is a **continuous** strategy space.
*   **Repeated Game:** A strategy is a complete plan of action specifying the quantity to produce in period $t$ based on the entire history of play up to $t-1$.

**3. Payoffs:**
*   **Objective:** Maximize the present value of the stream of profits: $\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \delta^t \pi_{it}$, where $\delta$ is the discount factor (representing the "patience" of the firm or the probability the game continues).
*   **Payoff Function:** $\pi_i(q_i, q_j) = [P(q_i + q_j) - c]q_i$, where $P$ is the inverse demand function and $c$ is the marginal cost.

**4. Key Features:**
*   **Timing:** Simultaneous moves in each period.
*   **Incentives:** There is a tension between the **Collusive outcome** (joint profit maximization) and the **Nash incentive** (unilateral deviation to increase individual profit).

---

### Part 2: Nash Equilibrium Analysis

In a repeated Cournot game, there are two primary types of Nash Equilibria: the repetition of the static Cournot-Nash equilibrium and the "Collusive" equilibrium sustained by trigger strategies.

#### 1. The Static Cournot-Nash Equilibrium (Repeated)
*   **Strategy Profile:** Both firms produce the Cournot quantity $q_C = \frac{a-c}{3b}$ in every period, regardless of history.
*   **Why it’s a Nash Equilibrium:** In any single period, if Firm B produces $q_C$, Firm A’s best response is $q_C$. Since this holds for every period, neither firm has an incentive to deviate unilaterally.
*   **Classification:** Pure Strategy Equilibrium.
*   **Stability and Likelihood:** **Highly Stable.** This is the "default" equilibrium. It does not require trust or a high discount factor. It is the unique equilibrium if the game is finitely repeated with a known end date (due to backward induction).

#### 2. The Collusive Equilibrium (Trigger Strategy)
*   **Strategy Profile (e.g., Grim Trigger):** 
    *   *Period 1:* Produce the monopoly share $q_M = \frac{a-c}{4b}$ (which is less than $q_C$).
    *   *Period $t$:* Produce $q_M$ if the opponent has always produced $q_M$ in the past. If the opponent has ever deviated, produce $q_C$ forever (the "punishment").
*   **Why it’s a Nash Equilibrium:** If the discount factor $\delta$ is high enough (typically $\delta \ge 9/17$ in standard linear models), the long-term benefit of maintaining high profits outweighs the one-time gain from "cheating" and the subsequent loss during the punishment phase.
*   **Classification:** Pure Strategy Equilibrium (in the context of the repeated game).
*   **Stability and Likelihood:** **Conditionally Stable.** It is only an equilibrium if firms are sufficiently patient ($\delta$ is high) and the threat of punishment is credible.

---

### Part 3: Discussion of Multiple Equilibria

**Coordination Problems:**
The firms face a coordination problem. While both prefer the Collusive Equilibrium, they fear the other might "cheat" (produce more to capture more market share). Without communication, firms may default to the Static Cournot-Nash equilibrium to avoid being exploited.

**Pareto Dominance:**
*   The **Collusive Equilibrium Pareto dominates** the Static Cournot-Nash Equilibrium. Both firms earn strictly higher profits under collusion than they do in the Cournot-Nash outcome.
*   However, the Cournot-Nash equilibrium is "risk-dominant" in many contexts because it does not rely on the cooperation of the other party to avoid a disastrously low payoff.

**Which is most likely to occur?**
*   **In Finitely Repeated Games:** The Static Cournot-Nash equilibrium is the only Subgame Perfect Nash Equilibrium (SPNE) due to the "unraveling" effect (firms cheat in the last period, therefore they cheat in the second-to-last, and so on).
*   **In Infinitely/Indefinitely Repeated Games:** Collusion becomes highly likely if:
    1.  The number of firms is small (easier to monitor).
    2.  The market is stable (predictable demand).
    3.  The discount factor $\delta$ is high (firms value future profits).
*   **Likelihood:** In a duopoly, firms often move toward "Tacit Collusion" (the Collusive Equilibrium) because the mutual gains are significant and the monitoring of a single competitor is relatively easy.

## Dominant Strategies Analysis
<details><summary>Full Analysis</summary>

This analysis examines a **Repeated Cournot Duopoly**, where two firms (A and B) compete by choosing quantities ($q_A, q_B$) simultaneously in each period, with the market price determined by the total output ($Q = q_A + q_B$).

---

### Part 1: Game Structure Analysis

1.  **Game Type**:
    *   **Non-cooperative**: Firms act in their own self-interest; any "collusion" must be self-enforcing (e.g., through trigger strategies).
    *   **Repeated**: This is not a one-shot interaction. The game is played over multiple periods ($t = 1, 2, \dots, T$ or $\infty$). This allows for reputation building, rewards, and punishments.
    *   **Simultaneous (within periods)**: In each round, firms choose quantities without knowing the other's choice for that specific round.
    *   **Continuous**: Strategy spaces are typically continuous (firms can choose any non-negative quantity).

2.  **Strategy Spaces**:
    *   **One-shot Strategy**: A single quantity choice $q_i \in [0, \infty)$.
    *   **Repeated Strategy**: A complete plan of action that dictates the quantity to produce in period $t$ based on the entire history of play up to $t-1$. Examples include "Always Cournot," "Grim Trigger," or "Tit-for-Tat."

3.  **Characterize Payoffs**:
    *   **Objective**: Maximize the present value of the sum of profits over time: $V_i = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \delta^t \pi_{i,t}$, where $\delta$ is the discount factor (patience).
    *   **Payoff Function**: $\pi_i(q_i, q_j) = q_i \cdot [P(q_i + q_j) - c_i]$, where $P$ is the inverse demand function and $c_i$ is the marginal cost.

4.  **Key Features**:
    *   **Information**: Usually assumed **Perfect Information** regarding past moves (each firm sees what the other produced in previous rounds) but **Imperfect Information** regarding the current move.
    *   **Timing**: Simultaneous moves in each stage.
    *   **Interdependence**: Firm A’s profit is strictly decreasing in Firm B’s quantity (and vice versa) because higher total supply lowers the market price.

---

### Part 2: Dominant Strategy Analysis

In a Cournot competition, the optimal choice for one firm depends entirely on the choice of the other. This leads to the following findings:

#### 1. Strictly Dominant Strategies
*   **None.**
*   **Reasoning**: There is no single quantity that is best regardless of the opponent's choice. If Firm B produces nothing ($q_B = 0$), Firm A’s best response is the monopoly quantity ($q_m$). If Firm B produces a very high quantity, Firm A’s best response is to produce a very small amount or exit the market. Because the "best" move changes based on the opponent's move, no strictly dominant strategy exists.

#### 2. Weakly Dominant Strategies
*   **None.**
*   **Reasoning**: For any quantity Firm A chooses, there is always a hypothetical quantity Firm B could choose that would make a different choice by Firm A more profitable.

#### 3. Dominated Strategies
While there are no dominant strategies, there are many **dominated** strategies (strategies that are always worse than another specific strategy):
*   **Quantities exceeding the "Best Response to Zero"**: Any quantity $q_i$ greater than the monopoly quantity $q_m$ is dominated.
    *   *Explanation*: If Firm B produces $q_B = 0$, $q_m$ is the profit-maximizing choice for Firm A. If Firm B produces $q_B > 0$, the market price drops. In this case, producing $q_m$ is still better than producing anything *larger* than $q_m$, because the larger quantity would further depress the price and increase total costs.
*   **Quantities where Price < Marginal Cost**: Any quantity so high that it pushes the market price below the marginal cost $c$ regardless of the opponent's move is dominated by producing $q=0$.

#### 4. Iteratively Eliminated Strategies
Through the process of **Iterated Deletion of Strictly Dominated Strategies (IDSDS)**, we can narrow the strategy space:
1.  **Step 1**: Eliminate all $q_i > q_m$ (as they are dominated by $q_m$).
2.  **Step 2**: Knowing the opponent will never produce more than $q_m$, Firm A will never produce less than the Best Response to $q_m$.
3.  **Step 3**: This iterative process continues, "shrinking" the range of rational quantities from both ends.
4.  **Convergence**: In a standard linear Cournot model, this iterative elimination converges to a single point: the **Cournot-Nash Equilibrium quantity ($q_{CN}$)**.

---

### Strategic Implications

1.  **The "Cournot Trap"**: In a one-shot game, the lack of a dominant strategy leads firms to the Nash Equilibrium, where they produce more and earn less than they would under a monopoly/collusive agreement.
2.  **The Power of Repetition**: Because this is a **repeated game**, the "Always Cournot" strategy (playing the Nash quantity every round) is a subgame perfect equilibrium, but it is not the only one.
3.  **Incentive to Collude**: Since $q_{collusive} < q_{CN}$, firms have an incentive to restrict output to monopoly levels. However, because $q_{collusive}$ is not a dominant strategy, both firms face a "Prisoner's Dilemma" logic in every round: they want to cheat on the agreement to capture more market share.
4.  **Trigger Strategies**: The analysis of dominated strategies explains why "Grim Trigger" works. By threatening to play the Cournot-Nash quantity (the result of iterative elimination) forever if the other firm cheats, firms can make cooperation ($q_{collusive}$) a rational choice, provided they value future profits enough (high $\delta$).
5.  **Predictability**: The fact that we can iteratively eliminate strategies toward the Nash Equilibrium suggests that in the absence of high trust or long-term horizons, the market will inevitably gravitate toward the Cournot-Nash output levels, resulting in lower prices for consumers but sub-optimal profits for the firms.
