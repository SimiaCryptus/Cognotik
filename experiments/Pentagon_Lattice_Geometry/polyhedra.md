# Polyhedral Extensions: Replicative Generators in Three-Dimensional Space

## Overview

The frameworks developed in `idea.md` and `affine.md` for multi-sheeted pentagon
tilings and generalized replicative generators from polygons extend naturally into three
dimensions. The central question becomes: given a **polyhedron** $Q$ with $f$ faces,
$e$ edges, and $v$ vertices, what is the structure of the expansion family generated by
reflections and half-turns about face centers and edge midpoints, and under what
conditions does this family reconnect into a well-defined 3D tiling, a 3D quasicrystal,
or a multi-sheeted covering space of $\mathbb{R}^3$?

The answer mirrors the 2D theory almost exactly, with one crucial new ingredient: the
**Euler characteristic** $\chi = v - e + f$ of the polyhedron plays the role that the
interior angle sum played for polygons, and the **dihedral angles** replace the interior
angles as the primary source of geometric frustration.

---

## 1. From Polygons to Polyhedra: The Structural Analogy

### 1.1 Generators from Faces and Edges

For a polyhedron $Q$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$, the natural generator operations are:

1. **Reflection across a face plane**: The affine map that reflects $\mathbb{R}^3$ through
   the plane containing face $f_k$, mapping $Q$ to an adjacent copy sharing that face.
2. **Half-turn about an edge axis**: Rotation by $\pi$ about the line containing edge
   $e_k$, mapping $Q$ to an adjacent copy sharing that edge.
3. **Rotation about a vertex**: For vertex-transitive polyhedra, rotations about vertex
   axes by angles $2\pi/n$ (where $n$ is the vertex valence) generate additional
   adjacency relations.

Each face reflection is an element of $O(3)$ with determinant $-1$ (an improper rotation),
while each edge half-turn is a proper rotation with determinant $+1$. Together, the
generators span a subgroup of $\text{Aff}(\mathbb{R}^3)$, the **3D affine group**.

The algebraic field $\mathbb{F}$ is now determined by the vertex coordinates of $Q$ in
$\mathbb{R}^3$:

| Polyhedron           | Field $\mathbb{F}$                               | Notes                                       |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------- |
| Cube                 | $\mathbb{Q}$                                     | All rational; tiles $\mathbb{Z}^3$          |
| Tetrahedron          | $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ or $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3})$ | Depends on embedding                        |
| Octahedron           | $\mathbb{Q}$                                     | Dual to cube; same field                    |
| Regular dodecahedron | $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$                           | 3D analogue of pentagon                     |
| Regular icosahedron  | $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$                           | Same field as dodecahedron                  |
| Truncated octahedron | $\mathbb{Q}$                                     | Tiles $\mathbb{R}^3$ (Kelvin structure)     |
| Rhombic dodecahedron | $\mathbb{Q}$                                     | Dual to cuboctahedron; tiles $\mathbb{R}^3$ |
| Regular 24-cell (4D) | $\mathbb{Q}$                                     | Self-dual; tiles $\mathbb{R}^4$             |

### 1.2 Dihedral Angles as the Frustration Measure

The 3D analogue of the interior angle sum condition is the **dihedral angle sum** around
each edge of the tiling. For a polyhedron $Q$ with dihedral angle $\alpha$ at each edge,
the number of copies that can meet at a single edge without gap or overlap is:

$$n_{\text{edge}} = \frac{2\pi}{\alpha}$$

For this to yield a valid flat tiling of $\mathbb{R}^3$, $n_{\text{edge}}$ must be a
positive integer for **every** edge of $Q$. This is the 3D analogue of the polygon
interior angle condition $\frac{2\pi}{\theta} \in \mathbb{Z}$.

The dihedral angles of the five Platonic solids are:

| Polyhedron   | Dihedral Angle $\alpha$                     | $2\pi/\alpha$  | Tiles $\mathbb{R}^3$? |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------- | -------------- | --------------------- |
| Tetrahedron  | $\arccos(1/3) \approx 70.53^\circ$          | $\approx 5.10$ | No                    |
| Cube         | $90^\circ = \pi/2$                          | $4$            | **Yes**               |
| Octahedron   | $\arccos(-1/3) \approx 109.47^\circ$        | $\approx 3.29$ | No (alone)            |
| Dodecahedron | $\arccos(-1/\sqrt{5}) \approx 116.57^\circ$ | $\approx 3.09$ | No                    |
| Icosahedron  | $\arccos(-\sqrt{5}/3) \approx 138.19^\circ$ | $\approx 2.60$ | No                    |

Only the **cube** satisfies the integer condition alone. The tetrahedron and octahedron
together satisfy it (the tetrahedral-octahedral honeycomb), and the truncated octahedron
satisfies it by itself — these are the primary examples of space-filling polyhedra.

## 2. The 3D Reconnection Criteria

The two reconnection criteria from `affine.md` Section 2 generalize directly to 3D,
with one additional criterion arising from the richer topology of $\mathbb{R}^3$.

### 2.1 Criterion 1: Finite Orientation Group (3D Version)

The orientation group $\Gamma_3 = \langle R_1, R_2, \dots, R_f \rangle \subseteq O(3)$
generated by the face reflections and edge half-turns must be **finite**.

In 3D, the finite subgroups of $O(3)$ are fully classified (by the crystallographic
restriction theorem and its extensions):

- **Cyclic groups** $\mathbb{Z}_n$ (rotations about a single axis): arise for prisms.
- **Dihedral groups** $D_n$ (rotations + reflections about a single axis): arise for
  antiprisms and bipyramids.
- **Tetrahedral group** $T \cong A_4$ (order 12): symmetry of the tetrahedron.
- **Octahedral group** $O \cong S_4$ (order 24): symmetry of the cube/octahedron.
- **Icosahedral group** $I \cong A_5$ (order 60): symmetry of the dodecahedron/icosahedron.
- Their extensions with reflections: $T_h$, $T_d$, $O_h$, $I_h$ (orders 24, 24, 48, 120).

For the expansion family to reconnect, $\Gamma_3$ must be one of these finite groups.
Generic irregular polyhedra generate infinite, dense subgroups of $O(3)$, failing this
criterion.

**Practical test**: All dihedral angles of $Q$ must be rational multiples of $\pi$.
Equivalently, all $e^{i\alpha_k}$ must be roots of unity.

### 2.2 Criterion 2: Single Irrational Base (3D Version)

Identical to the 2D case: the field $\mathbb{F}$ of vertex coordinates must be of the
form $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ for a single algebraic $\alpha$. The 3D translation vectors
$\mathbf{b}_k$ (determined by face centers and edge midpoints) must all live in
$\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)^3$.

For the Platonic solids:

- **Cube, octahedron**: $\mathbb{F} = \mathbb{Q}$ (trivially satisfies Criterion 2).
- **Tetrahedron**: $\mathbb{F} = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ or $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3})$
  depending on orientation; satisfies Criterion 2.
- **Dodecahedron, icosahedron**: $\mathbb{F} = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$; satisfies
  Criterion 2.

### 2.3 Criterion 3: Dihedral Angle Integrality (New in 3D)

This criterion has no direct 2D analogue. For a flat tiling of $\mathbb{R}^3$, the
dihedral angles at every edge of the assembled tiling must sum to exactly $2\pi$. This
requires:

$$\sum_{k} \alpha_k = 2\pi$$

where the sum is over all copies of $Q$ meeting at a given edge. If this sum is not
$2\pi$ for any integer number of copies, the polyhedron cannot tile $\mathbb{R}^3$ flatly,
and the expansion either:

- **Fails to reconnect** (tree structure), or
- **Reconnects on a multi-sheeted covering space** of $\mathbb{R}^3$ (the 3D analogue of
  the pentagon construction in `idea.md`).

The dodecahedron is the canonical example of the second case: its dihedral angle
$\approx 116.57^\circ$ does not divide $360^\circ$ evenly, yet the icosahedral symmetry
group is finite ($I_h$, order 120) and the field is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$. The
resolution, as in 2D, is a **multi-sheeted covering space of $\mathbb{R}^3$**.

## 3. Space-Filling Polyhedra and Periodic Lattices

### 3.1 The Five Primary Space-Fillers

The polyhedra that tile $\mathbb{R}^3$ by translation alone (or with rotations from a
finite crystallographic group) are well-studied. The primary examples relevant to our
framework are:

#### 3.1.1 The Cube ($\mathbb{Z}^3$ Lattice)

The cube is the 3D analogue of the square. Its expansion family is defined over
$\mathbb{F} = \mathbb{Q}$, the orientation group is $O_h$ (order 48, finite), and the
dihedral angle is exactly $90^\circ$, so four cubes meet at each edge ($4 \times 90^\circ
= 360^\circ$). The adjacency graph is the cubic lattice $\mathbb{Z}^3$ with
$d_{\text{eff}} = 3$ exactly.

The principal $G$-bundle structure is trivial: the fiber group is $G = \{e\}$ (no sheet
transitions needed), and the holonomy is everywhere zero.

#### 3.1.2 The Truncated Octahedron (Kelvin Structure)

The truncated octahedron (a polyhedron with 8 hexagonal faces and 6 square faces) is the
unique space-filler among the Archimedean solids. It tiles $\mathbb{R}^3$ in the
**bitruncated cubic honeycomb** (also called the Kelvin structure), which Lord Kelvin
conjectured in 1887 to be the minimal-surface partition of space into equal-volume cells.

The field is $\mathbb{F} = \mathbb{Q}$, the orientation group is $O_h$, and all dihedral
angles are rational multiples of $\pi$. The adjacency graph has $d_{\text{eff}} = 3$.

#### 3.1.3 The Rhombic Dodecahedron

The rhombic dodecahedron (12 rhombic faces, dual to the cuboctahedron) tiles $\mathbb{R}^3$
and arises naturally as the Voronoi cell of the face-centered cubic (FCC) lattice. Its
field is $\mathbb{F} = \mathbb{Q}$, and the adjacency graph is the FCC lattice with
coordination number 12.

#### 3.1.4 The Tetrahedral-Octahedral Honeycomb

The regular tetrahedron and regular octahedron together tile $\mathbb{R}^3$ in the ratio
2:1 (two tetrahedra per octahedron). This is the **tetrahedral-octahedral honeycomb**,
also known as the alternated cubic honeycomb. The field is $\mathbb{F} = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$
(from the octahedron's geometry), the orientation group is $O_h$, and the adjacency graph
is the pyrochlore lattice — a well-studied frustrated magnet geometry.

The dihedral angles satisfy:

- Tetrahedron: $\arccos(1/3) \approx 70.53^\circ$
- Octahedron: $\arccos(-1/3) \approx 109.47^\circ$
- At each shared edge: $70.53^\circ + 109.47^\circ = 180^\circ$ (one tet + one oct per
  edge in the honeycomb, but the full sum around each edge is $360^\circ$ with the
  correct multiplicity).

| --------------------------- | ---------------------- | ---------- | --- | --- | --- | ----------------------------- |
| Cube                        | $\mathbb{Q}$           | $O_h$      | ✓   | ✓   | ✓   | Cubic lattice ($d=3$)         |
| Truncated octahedron        | $\mathbb{Q}$           | $O_h$      | ✓   | ✓   | ✓   | Kelvin honeycomb ($d=3$)      |
| Rhombic dodecahedron        | $\mathbb{Q}$           | $O_h$      | ✓   | ✓   | ✓   | FCC Voronoi lattice ($d=3$)   |
| Tetrahedron + Octahedron    | $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ | $O_h$      | ✓   | ✓   | ✓   | Pyrochlore lattice ($d=3$)    |

## 4. The Dodecahedral Multi-Sheeted Covering Space

The regular dodecahedron is the 3D analogue of the regular pentagon: it satisfies
Criteria 1 and 2 but fails Criterion 3, making it the primary candidate for a
**multi-sheeted covering space of $\mathbb{R}^3$**.

### 4.1 The Dihedral Frustration

The dihedral angle of the regular dodecahedron is:
$$\alpha_{\text{dodec}} = \arccos\left(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\right) \approx 116.565^\circ$$

The number of dodecahedra that can meet at a single edge without gap or overlap is:
$$n = \frac{360^\circ}{116.565^\circ} \approx 3.09$$

This is not an integer. Three dodecahedra leave a gap of $\approx 10.3^\circ$, and four
would overlap by $\approx 106.3^\circ$. This is the direct 3D analogue of the pentagonal
angular deficit of $36^\circ$ in 2D.

### 4.2 The Covering Space Construction

Following the construction of `idea.md` Section 2.2, we define a covering space
$\mathcal{M}_3$ over $\mathbb{R}^3$. Each dodecahedron $D_{i,s}$ is identified by:

- Its centroid $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$ (with coordinates in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})^3$).
- Its orientation $R_i \in I_h$ (an element of the icosahedral symmetry group).
- Its sheet index $s \in G$ (a discrete fiber group).

The adjacency rule is identical to the 2D case: two dodecahedra share a face if and only
if their projected faces in $\mathbb{R}^3$ match identically in position and orientation,
with a sheet transition $\tau(f) \in G$ associated with each shared face.

### 4.3 The Fiber Group for the Dodecahedron

The fiber group $G$ is determined by the angular frustration. The dihedral deficit per
edge is:
$$\delta_{\text{dodec}} = 2\pi - 3\alpha_{\text{dodec}} \approx 10.3^\circ = \frac{\pi}{17.5}$$

This is not a rational multiple of $\pi$ in the simplest form, but within
$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$ it takes the exact value:
$$\delta_{\text{dodec}} = 2\pi - 3\arccos\left(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\right)$$

The minimal fiber group that accommodates this frustration while remaining finite is
related to the **120-cell** (the regular 4D polytope with dodecahedral cells), whose
symmetry group is the **600-cell group** of order 14400. This is the 3D analogue of the
pentagon's $\mathbb{Z}_5$ fiber group, but vastly richer in structure.

### 4.4 Connection to the 120-Cell and 4D Geometry

The 120-cell is the unique regular 4D polytope whose cells are regular dodecahedra. It
has:

- 120 dodecahedral cells
- 720 pentagonal faces
- 1200 edges
- 600 vertices

The 120-cell tiles the 3-sphere $S^3$ (the unit sphere in $\mathbb{R}^4$) exactly, with
4 dodecahedra meeting at each edge — which is possible because the dihedral angle of the
dodecahedron in the curved geometry of $S^3$ is exactly $120^\circ$, and
$3 \times 120^\circ = 360^\circ$.

This is the precise 3D analogue of the dodecahedron tiling the 2-sphere $S^2$ (as the
faces of the dodecahedron itself). The multi-sheeted covering space of $\mathbb{R}^3$ by
dodecahedra is therefore the **flat limit** of the 120-cell, obtained by "unrolling" the
curved 3-sphere geometry into flat $\mathbb{R}^3$ sheets — exactly as the pentagon
construction in `idea.md` unrolls the dodecahedron (tiling of $S^2$) into flat
$\mathbb{R}^2$ sheets.

### 4.5 Emergent Dimensionality

By the same argument as `idea.md` Section 4.2, the dodecahedral covering space has:
$$2 < d_{\text{eff}} < 4$$

The lower bound of 2 is trivial (the construction is at least 2D). The upper bound of 4
arises because the natural ambient space for the 120-cell is $\mathbb{R}^4$, and the
covering space cannot exceed the dimension of its natural regularization. The exact value
of $d_{\text{eff}}$ depends on the density and distribution of sheet transitions, which
is controlled by the cut-and-project window in a 6D ambient lattice (the natural lattice
for icosahedral quasicrystals, as opposed to the 5D lattice for pentagonal quasicrystals).

## 5. The Icosahedral Quasicrystal Connection

### 5.1 The 6D Ambient Lattice

Icosahedral quasicrystals — discovered experimentally by Shechtman in 1984 — are
3D structures with icosahedral symmetry that are aperiodic but long-range ordered. They
are most naturally described as cut-and-project structures from a 6D hypercubic lattice
$\mathbb{Z}^6$, with the physical 3D space embedded as an irrational 3D subspace of
$\mathbb{R}^6$.

The connection to our framework is direct:

- The **physical space** $E_\parallel \subset \mathbb{R}^6$ is the 3D subspace in which
  the quasicrystal lives.
- The **perpendicular space** $E_\perp \subset \mathbb{R}^6$ is the complementary 3D
  subspace, which plays the role of the "sheet direction."
- The **acceptance window** $W \subset E_\perp$ is a 3D icosahedron (or triacontahedron),
  which selects which lattice points project into the physical space.

In our multi-sheeted dodecahedral construction, the sheet index $s \in G$ is precisely
the coordinate in $E_\perp$, and the cut-and-project window $W$ is the acceptance window
that prevents the cyclotomic density trap (the 3D analogue of the 2D trap described in
`idea.md` Section 2.4).

### 5.2 The Ammann-Kramer-Neri Tiling

The 3D analogue of the Penrose tiling is the **Ammann-Kramer-Neri (AKN) tiling**, which
tiles $\mathbb{R}^3$ with two types of rhombohedra (prolate and oblate) in a quasiperiodic
pattern with icosahedral symmetry. The AKN tiling is the cut-and-project image of
$\mathbb{Z}^6$ with an icosahedral acceptance window.

The relationship between the AKN tiling and the dodecahedral multi-sheeted construction
is analogous to the relationship between the Penrose tiling and the pentagonal
multi-sheeted construction:

| 2D                         | 3D                          |
| -------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Regular pentagon           | Regular dodecahedron        |
| $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$     | $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$      |
| 5D ambient lattice         | 6D ambient lattice          |
| Penrose tiling             | AKN tiling                  |
| $\mathbb{Z}_5$ fiber group | Icosahedral fiber group $I$ |
| $2 < d_{\text{eff}} < 3$   | $3 < d_{\text{eff}} < 4$    |

### 5.3 Holonomy in 3D: From Spinors to Quaternions

In the 2D pentagon construction, the holonomy group is a subgroup of $SO(2) \cong U(1)$,
and the spinor analogy arises from the double cover $\mathbb{Z}_2 \to SO(2)$. In 3D, the
holonomy group is a subgroup of $SO(3)$, and the natural double cover is:
$$SU(2) \to SO(3)$$

The icosahedral symmetry group $I \cong A_5$ (order 60) lifts to the **binary icosahedral
group** $2I$ (order 120) in $SU(2)$. This group is isomorphic to the group of unit
icosians — elements of the form $a + bi + cj + dk$ with $a, b, c, d \in \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$
and $|a + bi + cj + dk| = 1$ — which are the quaternionic analogues of the 5th roots of
unity.

The holonomy of a loop in the dodecahedral covering space is therefore a **unit icosian**,
and the requirement of a $4\pi$ rotation to restore identity (the spinor condition) becomes
the requirement of traversing the loop **twice** in the covering space to return to the
same sheet. This is the precise 3D analogue of the spinor holonomy described in `idea.md`
Section 3.2.

## 6. Fractal Polyhedra and 3D Self-Similar Structures

### 6.1 The Menger Sponge

The Menger sponge is the 3D analogue of the Sierpiński carpet (itself the 2D analogue of
the Sierpiński triangle). It is constructed by repeatedly removing the central cube and
face-center cubes from a $3 \times 3 \times 3$ cube, leaving 20 sub-cubes at each
iteration.

In the framework of `affine.md`, the Menger sponge arises from the cube with a
**contraction ratio of $1/3$** and a specific removal rule. The field is
$\mathbb{F} = \mathbb{Q}$, the orientation group is $O_h$ (finite), and both Criteria 1
and 2 are satisfied. The fractal dimension is:
$$d_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\log 20}{\log 3} \approx 2.727$$

This places the Menger sponge in the quasi-reconnective regime, with $2 < d_{\text{eff}} < 3$.

### 6.2 The Sierpiński Tetrahedron (Tetrix)

The Sierpiński tetrahedron (or tetrix) is constructed by replacing a regular tetrahedron
with 4 smaller tetrahedra at its corners, each scaled by $1/2$. The fractal dimension is:
$$d_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\log 4}{\log 2} = 2$$

This is a degenerate case: despite being a 3D construction, the tetrix has the same
fractal dimension as a 2D surface. This reflects the fact that the tetrix is topologically
equivalent to a 2D fractal (the Sierpiński triangle) embedded in 3D space.

The field is $\mathbb{F} = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ (from the tetrahedron's geometry), the
orientation group is $T_d$ (finite), and both Criteria 1 and 2 are satisfied.

### 6.3 The 3D Koch Snowflake Analogues

Several 3D analogues of the Koch snowflake exist, constructed by replacing each face of a
polyhedron with a smaller copy of the polyhedron. The most natural is the **Koch
tetrahedron**, constructed by replacing each face of a regular tetrahedron with a smaller
tetrahedron pointing outward. The fractal dimension is:
$$d_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\log 6}{\log 3} \approx 1.631$$

Again, this is less than 2, reflecting the fact that the construction produces a fractal
surface rather than a fractal volume.

### 6.4 The 3D Fractal Taxonomy

| Structure                | $\mathbb{F}$           | $\Gamma_3$ | $d_{\text{eff}}$               | Type                  |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------- | ---------- | ------------------------------ | --------------------- |
| Menger sponge            | $\mathbb{Q}$           | $O_h$      | $\log 20/\log 3 \approx 2.727$ | 3D fractal volume     |
| Sierpiński tetrahedron   | $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ | $T_d$      | $\log 4/\log 2 = 2$            | Fractal surface in 3D |
| Koch tetrahedron         | $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ | $T_d$      | $\log 6/\log 3 \approx 1.631$  | Fractal surface       |
| Dodecahedral cover       | $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$ | $I_h$      | $3 < d_{\text{eff}} < 4$       | Multi-sheeted cover   |
| Icosahedral quasicrystal | $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$ | $I_h$      | $3$                            | Quasiperiodic ($d=3$) |

## 7. Cellular Automata on 3D Polyhedral Lattices

### 7.1 Generalizing the ICA to 3D

The Isometric Cellular Automaton (ICA) defined in `idea.md` Section 5 extends naturally
to 3D polyhedral lattices. For a polyhedron $Q$ with $f$ faces, each cell has exactly $f$
neighbors (one per face), and the natural rule families are **$f$-regular
outer-totalistic** rules parametrized by birth/survival sets on an $f$-neighbor count.

For the primary space-filling polyhedra:

- **Cube** ($f = 6$): 6-regular rules; includes the 3D analogue of Conway's Game of Life
  (studied by Carter Bays and others).
- **Truncated octahedron** ($f = 14$): 14-regular rules; richer neighborhood structure,
  potentially supporting more complex glider dynamics.
- **Rhombic dodecahedron** ($f = 12$): 12-regular rules; FCC neighborhood, relevant to
  crystal growth models.

### 7.2 Holonomy and Sheet-Jumping in 3D CA

For the dodecahedral multi-sheeted construction, the CA dynamics acquire a new feature:
**quaternionic sheet transitions**. When a glider crosses a face shared between two
dodecahedra on different sheets, its state is acted on by the corresponding unit icosian
$q \in 2I$. This is the 3D analogue of the spinor phase shift described in `idea.md`
Section 3.2.

The result is a CA in which:

- **Glider splitting** occurs at sheet boundaries, with the two components carrying
  conjugate icosian phases.
- **Vortex scattering** rotates glider trajectories by elements of the binary icosahedral
  group $2I$.
- **Topological protection**: gliders carrying non-trivial icosian phases cannot be
  annihilated by local rules that preserve the total icosian charge — a 3D analogue of
  topological protection in quantum computing.

### 7.3 Causal Structure and 3+1 Dimensional Extension

The causal extension of `idea.md` Section 5.2 becomes particularly natural in 3D: the
sheet stack is reinterpreted as a **foliation of 3D space-like slices** evolving along a
directed time axis, yielding a **4D causal cellular automaton**. The admissible
transitions are restricted to those respecting the causal ordering, converting the ICA
into a discrete model of 3+1 dimensional spacetime dynamics.

The dodecahedral multi-sheeted construction in this causal setting is a discrete toy model
for:

- **Loop quantum gravity**: the spin network states are replaced by icosian-valued
  holonomies on the dodecahedral adjacency graph.
- **Causal dynamical triangulations**: the causal foliation structure mirrors the CDT
  time-slicing, with the dodecahedral cells playing the role of 4-simplices.
- **Topological quantum field theory**: the icosian holonomy group $2I$ is the gauge
  group of a discrete $BF$-theory on the dodecahedral lattice.

## 8. Algorithmic Extensions to 3D

### 8.1 The 3D Adjacency Oracle

The adjacency oracle of `idea.md` Section 6.1 extends to 3D with the following
modifications:

```python
def get_neighbors_3d(cell_id):
    # All coordinates live in Q(sqrt(5))^3; comparisons are exact.
    # cell_id encodes: centroid (3 coords in Q(sqrt(5))),
    #                  orientation (element of I_h),
    #                  sheet index (element of fiber group G).
    faces = calculate_faces_exact(cell_id.coords, cell_id.orientation)
    neighbors = []
    for face in faces:
        # Find the unique dodecahedron whose matching face coincides
        # with this face in position and orientation.
        matching_cell = find_matching_face_3d(face, exclude_id=cell_id)
        if matching_cell and in_acceptance_window_6d(matching_cell):
            neighbors.append(matching_cell)
    return neighbors
```

The key difference from the 2D case is that face-matching in 3D requires checking both
the **position** and **orientation** of a pentagonal face in $\mathbb{R}^3$, which
involves 5 vertices each with 3 coordinates in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$ — a total of 15
rational pairs per face. The exact arithmetic requirement is therefore more demanding but
structurally identical.

### 8.2 The 6D Cut-and-Project Window

The cut-and-project window for the dodecahedral construction lives in a 6D ambient space
$\mathbb{Z}^6$, with the physical space $E_\parallel$ being a 3D irrational subspace and
the acceptance window $W \subset E_\perp$ being a 3D icosahedron or triacontahedron.

The window $W$ must be chosen to:

1. Guarantee local finiteness (only finitely many sheets pass through any compact region
   of $\mathbb{R}^3$).
2. Enforce icosahedral symmetry of the sheet-transition distribution.
3. Keep $d_{\text{eff}}$ bounded strictly inside $(3, 4)$.

The optimal window is the **triacontahedron** (the dual of the icosidodecahedron), which
is the natural Voronoi cell of the icosahedral lattice in $E_\perp$.

### 8.3 Spectral Analysis in 3D

The KPM spectral analysis of `idea.md` Section 6.4 extends directly to 3D. The graph
Laplacian $\Delta = D - A$ is now a sparse matrix with $D_{ii} = 12$ for interior
dodecahedral cells (12 faces per dodecahedron), and the density of states scaling:
$$\rho(\lambda) \sim \lambda^{d_{\text{spec}}/2 - 1} \quad \text{as } \lambda \to 0^+$$
yields $d_{\text{spec}}$ for the 3D construction. The expected range is:
$$3 < d_{\text{spec}} < d_{\text{eff}} < 4$$
with dimensional flow from $d_{\text{spec}} \approx 3$ in the UV (within a single sheet)
to a smaller IR value dominated by the icosahedral vortex bottlenecks.

### 9.2 Open Questions

1. **Exact value of $d_{\text{eff}}$ for the dodecahedral cover**: Can the effective
   dimension be computed analytically, or only numerically via KPM? Is it related to the
   dimension of the 120-cell's symmetry group in a simple way?

2. **Classification of 3D fiber groups**: For each of the five Platonic solids, what is
   the minimal fiber group $G$ that yields a locally finite, globally consistent covering
   space? Is there a general formula analogous to the 2D case?

3. **Universality of the $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$ field**: Both the pentagon (2D) and the
   dodecahedron/icosahedron (3D) are defined over $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$. Is this a
   coincidence, or does it reflect a deeper algebraic structure connecting 2D and 3D
   icosahedral geometry?

4. **4D extensions**: The 120-cell and 600-cell are the 4D analogues of the dodecahedron
   and icosahedron. Do they generate multi-sheeted covering spaces of $\mathbb{R}^4$ with
   $4 < d_{\text{eff}} < 5$? What is the ambient lattice dimension for the cut-and-project
   construction?

5. **Physical realization**: Can the dodecahedral multi-sheeted construction be realized
   as a physical material — e.g., a 3D quasicrystal with icosahedral symmetry and
   non-trivial holonomy? What would be the observable signatures of the fractional
   dimension and quaternionic holonomy in diffraction experiments or transport measurements?

6. **Computational complexity**: The 3D adjacency oracle requires exact arithmetic over
   $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})^3$ with 15 rational pairs per face. What is the computational
   complexity of the BFS expansion to radius $R$, and can it be parallelized efficiently
   on modern GPU architectures?
